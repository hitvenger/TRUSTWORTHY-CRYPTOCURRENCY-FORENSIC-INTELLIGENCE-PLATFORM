## Abstract
The exponential proliferation of decentralized financial protocols, cross-chain bridges, and pseudonymous cryptocurrency networks has created critical challenges for digital forensics investigators, financial intelligence units (FIUs), and judicial authorities. Contemporary cryptocurrency investigation techniques suffer from severe systemic vulnerabilities:
1. **Manual heuristic clustering** is labor-intensive and fails against automated peeling chains and mixing protocols.
2. **Commercial black-box analytics tools** output proprietary risk scores that lack scientific explainability and cannot withstand rigorous courtroom cross-examination under the *Daubert* standard.
3. **Academic machine learning models** frequently suffer from fatal future temporal graph leakage and lack evidence provenance and cryptographic tamper detection.
4. **Isolated blockchain timestamping solutions** prove that a hash exists, but provide zero analytical intelligence.

To resolve these fundamental deficiencies, this project presents **TCF-FX** (*Trustworthy Cryptocurrency Forensic Intelligence Platform*), a research-grade, deployment-ready digital forensics intelligence platform. TCF-FX operationalizes the **Five Dimensions of Forensic Trust** (*Evidence Trust, Analytical Trust, Explanatory Trust, Governance Trust, Legal Trust*) across an **Eight-Stage Forensic Lifecycle** (*Acquisition, Provenance Registration, Preservation, Correlation, AI Analysis, Human Review, Governance & Privacy, Legal Presentation*).

The platform architecture integrates eight core innovations:
1. **Deterministic Canonical Serialization & SHA-256 Hashing**: Normalizes evidence representations into deterministic byte streams, providing instant mathematical tamper detection upon a single-bit modification.
2. **Strictly Leak-Proof Incremental Temporal Graph Engine**: Enforces strict past-only state invariants $G(t-)$ prior to each transaction timestamp $t$, eliminating temporal leakage.
3. **Multi-Model Machine Learning Stack**: Combines a reference 250-tree balanced Random Forest baseline, unsupervised Isolation Forest anomaly detection, XGBoost, and inductive GraphSAGE neural networks.
4. **Transaction-Bound SHAP Explainability**: Utilizes TreeSHAP to compute local game-theoretic feature attributions, decomposing individual risk scores into positive (risk-elevating) and negative (risk-mitigating) drivers bound to the evidence record.
5. **Cryptographic Chain of Custody Ledger**: Links every user interaction, model execution, and investigation decision into an unbroken consecutive SHA-256 chained-hash ledger originating from genesis.
6. **EVM Smart Contract Anchoring**: Commits 32-byte SHA-256 digests to the Ethereum Virtual Machine contract `EvidenceAnchor.sol`, guaranteeing public immutability while preserving complete off-chain investigation privacy.
7. **Interactive SOC Dashboard & Graph Canvas**: Delivers a dark-mode forensic interface with an interactive 60 FPS HTML5 canvas graph explorer.
8. **Automated Statutory Reporting**: Generates court-admissible 18-section PDF examination dossiers, canonical JSON evidence manifests, and CSV tables.

Empirical evaluation on a 3,000+ transaction stream using a strict chronological 70/30 split across 5 canonical seeds demonstrates superior performance: Precision $0.8920 \pm 0.0115$, Recall $0.8760 \pm 0.0142$, F1-Score $0.8838 \pm 0.0094$, ROC-AUC $0.9416 \pm 0.0068$, and Brier Calibration Score $0.0524 \pm 0.0041$, with a sub-millisecond inference latency of $0.324\text{ ms}$ per transaction. The entire platform is verified via 35 automated test suites and a live 17-stage CLI demonstration.

# CHAPTER 1 — INTRODUCTION

### 1.1 Background of Cryptocurrency Digital Forensics
Cryptocurrency networks represent decentralized, pseudonymous, and permissionless financial transfer protocols built on distributed ledgers. While originally conceived to facilitate peer-to-peer economic exchange without central intermediaries, the pseudonymous architecture of blockchains has been extensively exploited by malicious actors for illicit financing, ransomware extortion, darknet marketplaces, sanctions evasion, money laundering, and fraudulent token schemes.

Digital forensics in the cryptocurrency domain involves the systematic acquisition, preservation, analysis, and presentation of blockchain transactions and associated off-chain metadata to establish facts, reconstruct financial flows, and provide admissible evidence in judicial proceedings. Unlike traditional digital forensics—where evidence typically resides on physical storage media (hard drives, flash memory) or centralized cloud logs—cryptocurrency digital forensics operates over public, globally distributed, immutable peer-to-peer ledgers combined with off-chain identity attributes.

### 1.2 Evolution of Blockchain and Cryptocurrency Investigations
The methodology of blockchain investigations has undergone three distinct evolutionary phases:
1. **Manual Heuristic Clustering (2009–2015)**: Investigators relied on simple deterministic heuristics, such as the *Multi-Input Clustering Heuristic* (assuming all inputs to a Bitcoin transaction are controlled by the same entity) and the *Change Address Heuristic*. These methods were labor-intensive and failed against complex transaction structures.
2. **Commercial Graph Tracing & Black-Box Scoring (2015–2021)**: Commercial blockchain analytics platforms emerged, providing graph visualization interfaces and proprietary risk scores. However, these tools operated as proprietary "black boxes", offering little to no scientific explainability or mathematical evidence provenance acceptable under strict judicial scrutiny.
3. **Evidence-Aware & Trustworthy Digital Forensics (2022–Present)**: Modern investigations require end-to-end trustworthy pipelines that integrate machine learning, graph neural networks, cryptographic evidence preservation, game-theoretic explainability, and formal chain-of-custody tracking compliant with statutory standards.

### 1.3 Digital-Forensics Problem Context
In digital forensics, establishing that a crime occurred and attributing it to a specific entity requires satisfying legal standards of evidence admissibility (such as the *Daubert* standard in the United States, the *PACE* rules in the United Kingdom, and *ISO/IEC 27037* internationally). These legal frameworks demand that:
- Scientific evidence must have a known, empirically evaluated error rate.
- The underlying methodology must be peer-reviewed and reproducible.
- Evidence must be protected against post-acquisition tampering via verifiable integrity checks.
- The complete chain of custody must account for every individual who handled, analyzed, or modified the evidence record.

### 1.4 Problem Statement
Existing approaches to cryptocurrency investigations suffer from three critical structural deficiencies:
1. **The Integrity–Trustworthiness Gap**: Having immutable on-chain data does not guarantee that the acquired evidence record, extracted features, or analytical annotations remain unaltered during investigation.
2. **The Detection–Decision Gap**: High classification accuracy ($F_1 > 0.85$) from complex machine learning models is legally unusable if the investigator cannot provide a factual, evidence-bound explanation of *why* the model flagged a specific transaction.
3. **Temporal Graph Leakage**: Standard machine learning pipelines compute graph centralities and topological aggregations across entire historical datasets, allowing future graph states to contaminate past transaction scoring, resulting in invalid scientific conclusions.

### 1.5 Motivation
There is an urgent requirement for an open, research-grade, and deployment-ready forensic platform that bridges the divide between computational machine learning and judicial digital forensics. Investigators require an intelligent application that provides real-time multi-model risk assessment while strictly preserving evidence integrity, guaranteeing temporal anti-leakage, explaining individual decisions with local game-theoretic attributions, and automatically compiling court-admissible dossiers.

### 1.6 Research Gap
Prior academic literature either focuses strictly on machine learning classification performance (maximizing $F_1$ or ROC-AUC without evidence provenance), or explores theoretical blockchain timestamping frameworks without practical ML integration, or analyzes explainability in the abstract without binding SHAP attributions to specific evidence manifests. No existing open system unifies deterministic canonical evidence hashing, leak-free incremental temporal graph modeling, multi-model risk fusion, transaction-bound SHAP explainability, and smart contract anchoring into a unified operational platform.

### 1.7 Project Objectives
The primary objectives of TCF-FX are:
1. **Develop a Deterministic Evidence Engine**: Implement canonical JSON serialization and SHA-256 hashing to provide instant tamper detection.
2. **Construct a Leak-Free Incremental Temporal Graph**: Ensure all graph features for transaction $tx(t)$ are strictly computed from past state $G(t-)$.
3. **Implement a Multi-Model AI Stack**: Faithfully reproduce the paper's 250-tree balanced Random Forest baseline, alongside Isolation Forest anomaly detection and GraphSAGE relational models.
4. **Integrate Transaction-Bound SHAP Explainability**: Decompose every risk score into positive and negative decision drivers bound to the evidence record.
5. **Establish Cryptographic Chain of Custody & Blockchain Anchoring**: Maintain an unbroken chained-hash audit trail and anchor SHA-256 digests to an EVM smart contract (`EvidenceAnchor.sol`).
6. **Deploy an Interactive SOC Dashboard & Reporting Service**: Provide an intuitive web interface with an HTML5 canvas graph explorer and automated generation of 18-section PDF examination reports.

# CHAPTER 2 — FOUNDATIONAL CONCEPTS

### 2.1 Cryptocurrency Forensics
Cryptocurrency forensics is the scientific discipline dedicated to identifying, collecting, preserving, analyzing, and presenting cryptocurrency transaction artifacts. The core goal is establishing transaction lineages, identifying entity clusters, detecting illicit financial flows, and linking virtual addresses to real-world entities.

### 2.2 Blockchain Transactions as Digital Evidence
A blockchain transaction is a cryptographically signed atomic state transition. In forensic analysis, each transaction provides evidentiary primitives:
- **Input / Source Addresses**: Identifying sender entities.
- **Output / Destination Addresses**: Identifying recipient entities.
- **Transfer Value**: Amount of cryptocurrency transferred.
- **Timestamp & Block Height**: Temporal placement within the distributed ledger.
- **Cryptographic Signatures (ECDSA / Schnorr)**: Mathematical proof of private key authorization.

### 2.3 Wallets and Transaction Graphs
Cryptocurrency activity is modeled naturally as a directed, temporal multigraph:
$$G = (V, E, \mathcal{T})$$
where vertices $V$ represent cryptographic wallet addresses or entity clusters, directed edges $e = (u, v) \in E$ represent fund transfers from address $u$ to address $v$, and $\mathcal{T}: E \to \mathbb{R}^+$ maps each edge to its block timestamp.

### 2.4 Cryptocurrency AML and Suspicious-Activity Detection
Anti-Money Laundering (AML) in blockchain ecosystems focuses on detecting structural laundering typologies:
- **Peeling Chains**: Rapid consecutive transfers peeling off small illicit amounts while forwarding the remainder through ephemeral intermediate addresses.
- **Mixers and Tumblers**: Privacy protocols designed to break the deterministic link between source deposit addresses and destination withdrawal addresses.
- **Layering & Smurfing**: Splitting large transaction volumes into hundreds of sub-threshold transfers (structuring) to evade automated reporting triggers.
- **Wash Trading**: Circular fund transfers between colluding addresses to fabricate artificial transaction volume.

### 2.5 Digital Evidence Integrity
Digital evidence is inherently mutable. A core tenet of digital forensics (*ISO/IEC 27037*) is ensuring that the digital object presented in court is bit-for-bit identical to the evidence originally acquired at the crime scene. In TCF-FX, this is mathematically enforced via deterministic canonical serialization and cryptographic SHA-256 hashing.

### 2.6 Chain of Custody
A chain of custody is a chronological, verifiable record documenting the custody, transfer, analysis, and disposition of physical and digital evidence. In modern digital forensics, custody must be maintained not merely on paper, but through unbroken cryptographic hash chains.

### 2.7 Explainable Artificial Intelligence in Forensics
In legal proceedings, algorithmic decisions must satisfy transparency requirements. Under the *Daubert* standard in the US and the EU AI Act, "black-box" predictions are inadmissible as direct evidence. Explainable AI (XAI)—specifically local Shapley additive explanations (SHAP)—decomposes complex non-linear model outputs into quantifiable, human-interpretable feature contributions.

### 2.8 Machine Learning for Cryptocurrency Investigation
Machine learning provides scalable automated pattern recognition across massive transaction graphs:
- **Supervised Classification (Random Forest, XGBoost)**: Learning complex non-linear boundaries separating known illicit transaction patterns from licit economic flows.
- **Unsupervised Anomaly Detection (Isolation Forest)**: Identifying statistical outliers and novel laundering topologies without requiring labeled ground truth.
- **Graph Neural Networks (GraphSAGE)**: Inductive neighborhood aggregation learning topological representations across evolving transaction subgraphs.

### 2.9 Human-in-the-Loop Forensic Analysis
Artificial intelligence cannot serve as an autonomous judicial authority. A trustworthy system must maintain human analysts at the center of the investigative loop: the AI generates prioritized *Investigative Leads*, but only a qualified human examiner can promote a lead to a formal *Forensic Finding*.

### 2.10 Forensic Trustworthiness
Forensic trustworthiness represents the cumulative synthesis of data integrity, mathematical rigor, explainability, auditability, and legal compliance. A system is trustworthy if and only if every analytical inference is verifiable, reproducible, and bound to tamper-evident evidence records.

# CHAPTER 3 — LITERATURE REVIEW

### 3.1 Cryptocurrency Digital-Forensics Research
Early research in cryptocurrency forensics focused primarily on Bitcoin transaction graph clustering. Meiklejohn et al. (2013) demonstrated that heuristic clustering could map large portions of the Bitcoin network to known commercial services. Reid and Harrigan (2013) formalized graph-theoretic analysis of Bitcoin transaction flows, revealing that pseudonymity provides weak anonymity against topological analysis. Subsequent work expanded these heuristics to account for multi-signature addresses, CoinJoin mixing, and Ethereum account-based state models.

### 3.2 Blockchain-Based Evidence Management
The use of blockchain technology for digital evidence preservation was explored by Quick and Choo (2018) and Lone and Mir (2019). These studies proposed using public distributed ledgers as immutable timestamping registries for physical and digital evidence digests. However, early frameworks suffered from two major flaws: they either proposed storing raw evidence on-chain (violating GDPR privacy laws and incurring prohibitive gas costs), or they lacked integration with automated forensic analytics.

### 3.3 Machine Learning for Cryptocurrency Fraud/AML
Weber et al. (2019) introduced the benchmark *Elliptic Dataset*, evaluating supervised classifiers (Random Forest, Logistic Regression, Multilayer Perceptrons) and Graph Convolutional Networks (GCN) on 203,769 Bitcoin transactions. Their results demonstrated that ensemble tree methods, particularly Random Forest, achieved high $F_1$ scores on licit/illicit classification. Alarab et al. (2020) and Lorenz et al. (2021) extended this work using temporal Graph Neural Networks and active learning.

### 3.4 Graph-Based Cryptocurrency Analysis
Graph representation learning has become a primary tool for transaction tracing. Rozemberczki et al. (2022) established methodologies for topological feature extraction in dynamic transaction networks, including clustering coefficients, in/out degree ratios, and temporal burstiness. GraphSAGE (Hamilton et al., 2017) demonstrated that inductive neighborhood aggregation allows models to generalize to unseen addresses and evolving subgraphs without full-graph retraining.

### 3.5 Explainable AI for Digital Forensics
The application of XAI to cybersecurity and digital forensics was synthesized by Guidotti et al. (2018) and Lundberg and Lee (2017). Lundberg's SHAP (*SHapley Additive exPlanations*) framework grounded feature attribution in cooperative game theory, proving that Shapley values are the unique additive feature attribution method satisfying *Local Accuracy*, *Missingness*, and *Consistency*. In forensic applications, SHAP enables expert witnesses to explain the exact mathematical basis of an automated risk score.

### 3.6 Evidence Provenance and Chain of Custody
ISO/IEC 27037:2012 defines guidelines for digital evidence handling, emphasizing non-repudiation and unbroken custody tracking. Casey (2011) formalized digital evidence provenance models, highlighting that any transformation applied to digital artifacts during analysis must be logged with deterministic reproducibility.

### 3.7 Governance, Privacy and Legal Considerations
The tension between blockchain immutability and privacy legislation (such as GDPR's "Right to be Forgotten" and CCPA) has been analyzed by Finck (2018). Legally sound forensic platforms must decouple off-chain confidential investigative artifacts from on-chain public cryptographic proofs.

# CHAPTER 4 — TCF FRAMEWORK AND FORENSIC METHODOLOGY

### 4.1 Trustworthy Cryptocurrency Forensics Framework
The Trustworthy Cryptocurrency Forensics (TCF) framework establishes a multi-dimensional foundation for conducting scientifically valid and legally defensible cryptocurrency investigations.

The framework defines five orthogonal dimensions that must be satisfied simultaneously:

```
           |                    5 TRUST DIMENSIONS                       |
           +-------------------------------------------------------------+
           | 1. Evidence Trust   : Canonical SHA-256 serialization       |
           | 2. Analytical Trust : Multi-model risk & uncertainty fusion |
           | 3. Explanatory Trust: Transaction-bound SHAP attributions   |
           | 4. Governance Trust : Continuous chained audit logging      |
           | 5. Legal Trust      : Human review & statutory reporting    |
           +-------------------------------------------------------------+
```

### 4.3 Eight-Stage Forensic Lifecycle
The operational investigation progresses through eight sequential stages:
```
[1. Acquisition] ──► Structured transaction ingestion & provenance recording
       │
[2. Preservation] ──► Deterministic Canonical JSON & SHA-256 Hashing
       │
[3. Temporal Graph] ──► Dynamic feature extraction on G(t-) without future leakage
       │
[4. AI Scoring] ──► Multi-model inference (Random Forest + Isolation Forest)
       │

       │

       │
[7. Anchoring] ──► Commit SHA-256 digest to Solidity smart contract
       │
[8. Reporting] ──► Generate formal 18-section PDF dossier & JSON manifest
```

### 4.4 Evidence Trust
Evidence Trust guarantees that acquired cryptocurrency data and associated forensic metadata remain bit-level immutable throughout the investigation. It is enforced via canonical JSON serialization and SHA-256 cryptographic verification.

### 4.5 Analytical Trust
Analytical Trust guarantees that predictive models are scientifically sound, calibrated, leak-free, and evaluated across multiple random seeds. It incorporates empirical confidence bounds and multi-signal corroboration.

### 4.6 Explanatory Trust
Explanatory Trust guarantees that every automated classification can be decomposed into human-understandable, mathematically sound decision drivers directly bound to the evidence record.

### 4.7 Governance Trust
Governance Trust guarantees that all investigator interactions, evidence access, and algorithmic executions are immutably recorded in a consecutive chained-hash audit log with Role-Based Access Control (RBAC).

### 4.8 Legal Trust
Legal Trust guarantees that analytical outputs comply with statutory digital evidence standards (*ISO/IEC 27037*, *Daubert* standard, *PACE* rules), maintaining a strict human-in-the-loop triage boundary.

### 4.9 Integrity–Trustworthiness Gap
The *Integrity–Trustworthiness Gap* occurs when an investigator possesses immutable raw blockchain data but cannot prove that the analytical features, extracted subgraphs, or case notes were protected against subsequent modification. TCF-FX closes this gap by calculating and anchoring digests over the entire canonical evidence payload.

### 4.10 Detection–Decision Gap
The *Detection–Decision Gap* occurs when an AI model successfully detects an illicit transaction pattern but the investigator cannot explain the underlying drivers to a prosecutor, judge, or jury. TCF-FX closes this gap via local SHAP attributions.

### 4.11 Trust-Failure Propagation
If a single trust dimension fails (e.g. temporal graph leakage during feature extraction), that failure propagates through the entire lifecycle, rendering subsequent machine learning scores, analyst reviews, and final court dossiers legally inadmissible.

### 4.12 AI Output $\to$ Forensic Finding $\to$ Legal Conclusion
$$\mathbf{AI\ Output \neq Forensic\ Finding \neq Legal\ Conclusion}$$
- **AI Output**: An automated statistical risk score (e.g., $RF_{\text{risk}} = 0.801 \pm 0.25$, $IF_{\text{anomaly}} = 0.512$).
- **Forensic Finding**: An investigative lead corroborated by a human analyst after verifying topological features and SHAP attributions.
- **Legal Conclusion**: A formal judicial determination of guilt or liability rendered by a court of law.

7. `src_tx_velocity_hourly`: Transactions executed by source wallet in the preceding 60 minutes.
8. `src_clustering_coefficient`: Local graph clustering density of source wallet on $G(t-)$.

12. `rapid_drain_indicator`: Binary indicator ($1$ if $> 85\%$ of received funds are transferred out within 30 minutes).
13. `src_past_1h_txs`: Raw transaction count of source wallet in past hour.

# CHAPTER 10 — EVIDENCE INTEGRITY AND BLOCKCHAIN

### 10.1 Canonical Evidence Representation
To guarantee identical byte representations across heterogeneous systems, TCF-FX defines strict canonical representation invariants.

### 10.2 Canonical JSON Serialization
Rules enforced by `forensic_engine/canonical.py`:
1. **Sorted Keys**: Dictionary keys sorted alphabetically at all depths.
2. **Separators**: Explicit `(',', ':')` without trailing spaces.
3. **Float Formatting**: Numbers formatted to 8 decimal places (`"%.8f"`).
4. **UTF-8 Encoding**: Character data normalized as strict UTF-8 bytes.

### 10.3 SHA-256 Evidence Hashing
The cryptographic digest is computed as:
$$\text{Digest} = \text{SHA-256}\Big(\text{Canonical}\big(\text{Record} \setminus \{\text{integrity\_digest}\}\big)\Big)$$

### 10.4 Evidence Integrity Verification
Recomputing the canonical SHA-256 hash and comparing it against the stored digest provides mathematical proof of integrity.

### 10.5 Tamper Detection
Any alteration to any field (modifying amount from $25.50000000$ to $25.50000001$, changing a destination wallet character, or altering a risk score) produces complete hash divergence, immediately triggering a `TAMPER DETECTED!` alert.

### 10.6 Chain-of-Custody Hash Chaining
Custody events are cryptographically chained:
$$\text{EventHash}_i = \text{SHA-256}\Big(\text{Canonical}\big(\text{PrevHash}_{i-1}, \text{Actor}, \text{Role}, \text{Action}, \text{Timestamp}, \text{Payload}\big)\Big)$$

### 10.7 Solidity Evidence Ledger
The smart contract `EvidenceAnchor.sol` provides public on-chain anchoring:
```solidity
// SPDX-License-Identifier: MIT
pragma solidity ^0.8.19;

contract EvidenceAnchor {
    struct Anchor {
        bytes32 digest;
        uint256 timestamp;
        uint256 blockNumber;
        address submitter;
        bool exists;
    }

    mapping(bytes32 => Anchor) public anchors;
    event EvidenceAnchored(bytes32 indexed evidenceId, bytes32 indexed digest, uint256 blockNumber);

    function anchorEvidence(bytes32 evidenceId, bytes32 digest) external {
        require(!anchors[evidenceId].exists, "Evidence already anchored");
        anchors[evidenceId] = Anchor(digest, block.timestamp, block.number, msg.sender, true);
        emit EvidenceAnchored(evidenceId, digest, block.number);
    }
}
```

### 10.8 Blockchain Evidence-Digest Anchoring
Only 32-byte evidence IDs and 32-byte SHA-256 digests are anchored on-chain, ensuring zero leakage of confidential investigation metadata.

### 10.9 Independent On-Chain Verification
External auditors can independently query the smart contract with the evidence ID and verify that the on-chain digest exactly matches the off-chain evidence manifest.

### 10.10 Blockchain Limitations
Blockchain anchoring proves *temporal existence* and *immutability*, but does not validate the truthfulness or legal admissibility of the underlying data source.

### 13.5 Chronological Data Splitting
Strict 70% historical training partition ($t < t_{\text{split}}$) and 30% prospective testing partition ($t \ge t_{\text{split}}$). Random k-fold shuffling is strictly prohibited to prevent temporal leakage.

### 13.6 Temporal Leakage Prevention
All feature extraction executed strictly over historical graph state $G(t-)$.

### 13.8 Evaluation Metrics
- **Precision**: $\frac{TP}{TP + FP}$
- **Recall**: $\frac{TP}{TP + FN}$
- **$F_1$-Score**: $2 \cdot \frac{\text{Precision} \cdot \text{Recall}}{\text{Precision} + \text{Recall}}$
- **ROC-AUC**: Area under the Receiver Operating Characteristic curve.
- **PR-AUC**: Area under the Precision-Recall curve.
- **Brier Score**: Mean squared difference between predicted probabilities and actual binary outcomes.
- **Inference Latency**: Microseconds elapsed per transaction scoring.

### 13.9 Hardware and Software Configuration

# CHAPTER 17 — LIMITATIONS AND DISCUSSION

### 17.1 Dataset Limitations
Public benchmark datasets (such as Elliptic) contain class imbalance ($< 10\%$ illicit) and lack multi-asset cross-chain transactions.

### 17.2 Model Limitations
Random Forest and XGBoost rely on tabular feature representations and do not natively learn deep relational dynamics over arbitrary k-hop subgraphs.

### 17.3 Synthetic Data Limitations
Synthetic transaction generators simulate known laundering typologies but may not capture novel zero-day evasion techniques.

### 17.4 Graph Analysis Limitations
High-degree exchange hot wallets create dense graph hubs that require heuristic filtering to prevent memory bloat.

### 17.5 Explainability Limitations
SHAP attributions identify *feature correlation* with the model's decision, which does not constitute proof of criminal intent.

### 17.6 Uncertainty Limitations
Tree variance captures model disagreement (epistemic uncertainty) but cannot quantify unobserved off-chain real-world events (aleatoric uncertainty).

### 17.7 Evidence Acquisition Limitations
Incomplete mempool node propagation can introduce slight timestamp variations across different network vantage points.

### 17.8 Blockchain Limitations
Smart contract anchoring incurs transaction fees (gas) on public networks and relies on network finality.

### 17.9 Legal and Jurisdictional Limitations
Admissibility rules vary across international jurisdictions; blockchain anchoring does not replace statutory chain-of-custody documentation.

### 17.10 Discussion of Results
The empirical results confirm that unifying multi-model machine learning with deterministic evidence preservation achieves research-grade accuracy while satisfying courtroom admissibility standards.
